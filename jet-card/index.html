Jet card guide

What is a jet card?

A jet card is prepaid private flying. You buy a block of flight hours — usually starting at 25 hours — at a fixed hourly rate, then draw down the balance as you fly. Think of it as a middle ground between booking one charter at a time and owning a share of an aircraft.

Horizon Jets does not run a jet card program. This page explains how cards work, so you can decide whether one makes sense for you — and if it does, we can help you compare providers' cards and find the right fit.

How a jet card works

You prepay for hours

A typical entry-level card starts at 25 hours. You pay upfront (or place a deposit), and each flight deducts the flown hours from your balance at the card's fixed rate.

The hourly rate is fixed

Unlike on-demand charter, where the price moves with availability and positioning, a jet card locks in your hourly rate for the life of the card. That rate is higher than a typical on-demand hourly rate — because it bundles fuel, tax, and guaranteed availability into one number.

Availability is guaranteed

Most cards promise an aircraft with a set amount of notice — often 24 to 48 hours, sometimes less — even at peak times. That guarantee is the main thing you're paying extra for.

You usually pick a category, not a specific jet

Cards are normally sold by aircraft category (light, midsize, super-midsize, and so on). You get an aircraft from that category, not a particular tail number.

What jet cards cost

Jet card hourly rates run higher than on-demand charter for the same aircraft category, because the rate includes fuel, tax, and the availability guarantee. As a rough guide, published averages for entry-level North American cards in mid-2026 were around $8,500 per hour for a light jet, $9,600 for a midsize jet, and $15,200 for a large-cabin jet, all-in.

A 25-hour light-jet card at those rates is roughly a $210,000 commitment. Actual card prices vary widely by provider, aircraft category, and terms — always compare the full contract, not just the hourly rate.

When a jet card makes sense — and when it doesn't

A jet card can make sense if you fly 25 or more hours a year, mostly in the same aircraft category, value guaranteed availability over the lowest price, and want predictable billing.

On-demand charter is usually better if you fly a few times a year, your trips vary a lot in distance or group size, or you want to compare prices across providers for each trip.

What to check before buying any jet card

  • Expiration. Many cards expire after 12–24 months, and unused hours may not be refunded.
  • Peak days and blackout dates. Some cards limit or surcharge flights on the busiest days.
  • Interchange. Can you swap to a different aircraft category, and at what rate?
  • The provider's finances. You're prepaying a large sum. Check how long the provider has operated and how customer funds are held.
  • Cancellation and refund terms. What happens if you want out early?
  • What's included. Confirm whether catering, de-icing, international fees, and ground transport are covered or extra.

The bottom line

A jet card buys certainty: a fixed rate and a guaranteed aircraft. You pay a premium for that certainty. If you fly often enough and value simplicity over shopping around, it can be worth it. If you fly occasionally, on-demand charter usually costs less per hour.

If you're not sure which fits, start with a charter quote for a real trip — it costs nothing and gives you a concrete price to compare against. And if a card does look right, ask us to help you compare providers' cards before you commit to one.
